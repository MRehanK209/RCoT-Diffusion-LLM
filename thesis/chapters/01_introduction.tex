\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models are often judged by a single generated answer, as if that
answer were a direct readout of reasoning ability. In practice, however, the
answer is the end point of a decoding procedure. Most current reasoning systems
use autoregressive decoding: the model writes from left to right, and each later
token conditions on choices already made
\cite{vaswani2017attention,brown2020language}. This interface is simple,
effective, and well supported by current inference systems, but it fixes early
tokens before the rest of the reasoning trace is known. Later tokens can extend
or explain the prefix; they do not naturally revise it.

For evaluation, that detail matters. A benchmark sees only the trace that was
generated, the answer extracted from it, and the final scoring decision. With
one sample, the score reflects one trajectory. With many samples, the score also
depends on whether at least one useful candidate appears somewhere in the set.
The same benchmark can therefore produce different rankings once the sampling
budget changes.

This budget dependence is clear in recent work on reinforcement learning with
verifiable rewards. Yue et al. report that RLVR-trained models can lead their
base models at small $k$, while the base models can reach higher pass@k when
$k$ is large \cite{yue2025rlvr}. The point is not simply that one model is
better than another. Top-1 performance and broad candidate-set coverage can
measure different behavior: a model shaped less strongly by post-training may
sample a wider range of reasoning attempts. That result motivates the question
studied here: if this low-$k$ versus high-$k$ split appears among RLVR and base
autoregressive models, does anything similar appear when the generation
interface is changed to diffusion?

The question is especially relevant for tasks with several constraints. In
Countdown-cd4, a model must use the provided numbers in a valid arithmetic
chain; a bad intermediate step can make the target unreachable. In Trip
Planning, the itinerary must satisfy city order, stay durations, and flight
transitions at the same time \cite{xie2024travelplanner}. GSM8K is a more
standard arithmetic benchmark, but here it also tests answer extraction because
the primary strict scorer expects a boxed final answer \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}.
These tasks make generation order, sample budget, and parser behavior part of
the measurement problem, not just implementation details.

Autoregressive models should not be treated as weak baselines. Chain-of-thought
prompting and self-consistency show that repeated sampling can recover from
individual failed traces by producing many candidate solutions
\cite{wei2022chain,wang2023selfconsistency}. The relevant question is therefore
not whether autoregressive models can reason. It is how their performance is
distributed across the evaluation budget: how much appears in the first decoded
solution, how much appears only after many stochastic samples under pass@k-style
evaluation \cite{chen2021humaneval}, and whether the additional samples solve
the same questions or different ones.

Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) provide a useful contrast because they
generate through masked or iterative denoising rather than strict next-token
commitment. A dLLM can begin from a masked or partially specified sequence and
refine multiple positions over a sequence of denoising steps, following the
discrete denoising view of text generation \cite{austin2021structured}. Recent
systems such as LLaDA and Dream scale this interface to large language models
\cite{nie2025llada,ye2025dream}. This interface suggests a different allocation
of test-time computation. It may help produce coherent first samples on tasks
where a plan must satisfy several constraints at once, or it may produce
candidate sets that behave differently under pass@k evaluation. These are
hypotheses about the decoding interface, not claims that diffusion models
should be superior in general.

The comparison is therefore not just between isolated checkpoints. It is a
comparison between two ways of turning a prompt into scored candidate answers.
AR models expose test-time search mainly through repeated left-to-right
sampling. dLLMs add choices inside the denoising process, such as sequence
length, block structure, and number of refinement steps. The experiments keep
the downstream scoring pipeline shared so that these generation choices are
judged by the same parsers and aggregate metrics.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
The evaluation is organized around one motivating question and two supporting
questions. The main question is \textbf{RQ1}: whether the diffusion runs show
the same large-$k$ coverage pattern reported by Yue et al., where weaker pass@1
performance can be offset by broader coverage when many samples are drawn.
\textbf{RQ2} provides the measurement frame by asking whether model rankings
change between greedy $n=1$ accuracy, low-budget sampling, and high-budget
pass@k on reasoning and planning tasks. \textbf{RQ3} is interpretive: it asks
how much parser compatibility and per-question complementarity help explain the
observed differences. Together, these questions treat deterministic scoring,
low-$k$ sampling, and high-$k$ pass@k as related but distinct evaluation
regimes, with RQ2 and RQ3 supporting the main RQ1 comparison.

The study compares two dLLM families, LLaDA and Dream
\cite{nie2025llada,ye2025dream}, against two AR families, Qwen2.5 and
Llama~3.1 \cite{qwen2024qwen25,grattafiori2024llama}. The benchmark set covers
GSM8K, Countdown-cd4, and Trip Planning. GSM8K and Trip Planning follow their
published definitions \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k,xie2024travelplanner}. Hereafter,
\emph{AR} denotes autoregressive models and \emph{dLLM} denotes diffusion large
language models. The evaluation combines deterministic scoring, stochastic
pass@k sampling, parser-based answer extraction, prompt-format ablations, and
per-question correctness analysis.

Each benchmark plays a different role. GSM8K checks arithmetic reasoning and
answer extraction in a setting where high-budget scores are close to saturated.
Countdown-cd4 makes the search aspect visible because a candidate must satisfy
exact arithmetic constraints before it can count as correct. Trip Planning
extends the same idea to longer natural-language plans, where an output can
fail because the plan is wrong or because the itinerary cannot be parsed.
Together, the tasks separate reasoning quality, sampling behavior, and format
compatibility more clearly than a single accuracy number would.

The empirical pattern is mixed. Countdown separates the models more clearly
than GSM8K, while Trip Planning is harder to interpret because parser failures
are common. In the planning settings, LLaDA and Dream are competitive or
leading at several low-$k$ budgets, while Qwen and Llama can gain more from
repeated stochastic sampling and overtake at higher pass@k. GSM8K gives a
different lesson: because high-$k$ scores are close to saturated, it mainly
exposes prompt-format and answer-extraction sensitivity. The success sets
overlap but are not identical, which makes hybrid systems worth studying.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
The work makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It provides a shared downstream evaluation of two diffusion model
    families and two autoregressive model families at similar scale across
    arithmetic reasoning, symbolic planning, and structured natural-language
    planning.
    \item It shows that model rankings can change with the sampling budget:
    LLaDA and Dream are competitive or leading in several low-$k$ settings,
    while Qwen and Llama overtake in some high-$k$ settings.
    \item It adds parser-sensitivity, normalized-answer diversity,
    per-question correlation, and oracle-union analyses to show where the
    paradigms overlap and where they remain complementary.
\end{itemize}
