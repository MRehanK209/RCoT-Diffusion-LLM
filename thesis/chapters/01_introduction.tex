\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models are often evaluated as if reasoning quality were a
property of a single generated answer. In practice, the answer is produced by a
decoding process. Most current language-model reasoning systems use
autoregressive decoding: they extend a prefix from left to right, and every
later token is conditioned on choices that have already been made
\cite{vaswani2017attention,brown2020language}. This interface is simple,
effective, and well supported by modern inference systems. It is also a
particular way of spending computation. Once an early token has been emitted,
later tokens can elaborate on it, but they do not naturally edit the prefix that
created the current reasoning path.

This matters because a reasoning benchmark only observes the answer a model
actually produces: a completed trace, an extracted answer, and a scoring
decision. If the trace is generated once, the evaluation rewards the quality of
a single trajectory. If many traces are generated, the evaluation also rewards
the model's ability to place at least one correct candidate in the sampled set.
These two views can lead to different rankings even when the underlying
benchmark is unchanged.

Recent work on reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards makes this
budget dependence explicit. Yue et al. find that RLVR-trained models can be
stronger than their base models at small $k$, while the corresponding base
models can obtain higher pass@k scores when $k$ is large
\cite{yue2025rlvr}. The interpretation is that top-1 benchmark performance
does not necessarily measure the same property as broad candidate-set
coverage: a model that is less concentrated by post-training may sample more
varied reasoning attempts. This observation motivates the central evaluation
question in this thesis. Instead of assuming that the best first answer also
defines the best model family, the experiments ask whether a similar
low-$k$ versus high-$k$ separation appears for diffusion and autoregressive
language models.

The issue becomes concrete on reasoning and planning tasks that combine local
steps with global constraints. In Countdown-cd4, a model must use the available
numbers in a valid arithmetic chain; one poor intermediate operation can leave
no useful route to the target. In Trip Planning, the output must satisfy a city
order, stay durations, and flight transitions at the same time
\cite{xie2024travelplanner}. GSM8K is more familiar as arithmetic word-problem
solving, but in this thesis it also tests answer-extraction behavior because
the primary strict scorer requires a boxed final answer \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}.
These examples show why generation order, sample budget, and parser behavior
are part of the measurement problem rather than incidental implementation
details.

Autoregressive models should not be treated as weak baselines. Chain-of-thought
prompting and self-consistency show that repeated sampling can recover from
individual failed traces by producing many candidate solutions
\cite{wei2022chain,wang2023selfconsistency}. The relevant question is therefore
not whether autoregressive models can reason. It is how their performance is
distributed across the evaluation budget: how much appears in the first decoded
solution, how much appears only after many stochastic samples under pass@k-style
evaluation \cite{chen2021humaneval}, and whether the additional samples solve
the same questions or different ones.

Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) provide a useful contrast because they
generate through masked or iterative denoising rather than strict next-token
commitment. A dLLM can begin from a masked or partially specified sequence and
refine multiple positions over a sequence of denoising steps, following the
discrete denoising view of text generation \cite{austin2021structured}. Recent
systems such as LLaDA and Dream scale this interface to large language models
\cite{nie2025llada,ye2025dream}. This interface suggests a different allocation
of test-time computation. It may help produce coherent first samples on tasks
where a plan must satisfy several constraints at once, or it may produce
candidate sets that behave differently under pass@k evaluation. These are
hypotheses about the decoding interface, not claims that diffusion models
should be superior in general.

The comparison is not between two isolated checkpoints, but between two ways of
turning a prompt into a set of scored candidate answers. AR models expose
test-time search mainly through repeated left-to-right samples. dLLMs expose
additional choices inside the denoising process, including the sequence length,
block structure, and number of refinement steps. The experiments keep the
downstream scoring pipeline shared so that these generation choices can be
examined through the same parsers and aggregate metrics.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
The thesis separates the evaluation into three questions.
\textbf{RQ1} asks whether model rankings change between greedy $n=1$ accuracy,
low-budget sampling, and high-budget pass@k on reasoning and planning tasks.
\textbf{RQ2} asks whether the evaluated diffusion configurations show the
Yue-style large-$k$ coverage pattern, where weaker pass@1 performance can be
offset by broader coverage when many samples are drawn. \textbf{RQ3} asks how
much parser compatibility and per-question complementarity explain the observed
differences. These questions treat the deterministic score, the low-$k$ sample
set, and the high-$k$ pass@k curve as related but distinct evaluation regimes.

The study compares two dLLM families: LLaDA and Dream
\cite{nie2025llada,ye2025dream}. The AR side uses Qwen2.5 and Llama~3.1
\cite{qwen2024qwen25,grattafiori2024llama}. The benchmark set covers GSM8K,
Countdown-cd4, and Trip Planning. GSM8K and Trip Planning follow their
published benchmark definitions \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k,xie2024travelplanner}.
Hereafter, \emph{AR} denotes autoregressive models and \emph{dLLM} denotes
diffusion large language models. The evaluation uses deterministic scoring,
stochastic pass@k sampling, parser-based answer extraction, prompt-format
ablations, and per-question correctness analysis.

The three benchmarks are used for different reasons. GSM8K checks arithmetic
reasoning and answer extraction in a setting where high-budget performance is
close to saturation. Countdown-cd4 makes the search aspect visible because a
candidate must satisfy exact arithmetic constraints before it can be counted as
correct. Trip Planning extends the same idea to longer natural-language plans,
where an output can fail because the plan is wrong or because the itinerary is
not parseable. Together, the tasks separate reasoning quality, sampling
behavior, and format compatibility more cleanly than a single
accuracy number would allow.

Countdown separates the models more clearly than GSM8K, while Trip Planning is
harder to interpret because parser failures are common. In the evaluated
planning settings, the evaluated diffusion configurations are competitive or
leading in several low-$k$ settings, while the evaluated Qwen/Llama
configurations can gain more from repeated stochastic sampling and overtake at
higher pass@k budgets. GSM8K gives a different lesson: because high-$k$ scores
are close to saturated, it mainly exposes prompt-format and answer-extraction
sensitivity. The success sets overlap but are not identical, so hybrid systems
may be worth studying.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This thesis makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It provides a shared downstream evaluation of two diffusion model
    families and two autoregressive model families at similar scale across
    arithmetic reasoning, symbolic planning, and structured natural-language
    planning.
    \item It shows that model rankings can change with the sampling budget:
    the evaluated diffusion configurations are competitive or leading in
    several low-$k$ settings, while the evaluated Qwen/Llama configurations
    overtake in some high-$k$ settings.
    \item It adds parser-sensitivity, normalized-answer diversity,
    per-question correlation, and oracle-union analyses to show where the
    paradigms overlap and where they remain complementary.
\end{itemize}
