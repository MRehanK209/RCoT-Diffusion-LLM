\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Autoregressive Language Models}
Autoregressive language models define sequence probability by predicting each
token from its prefix. Transformer decoders made this objective scalable by
combining causal self-attention with highly parallel training over token
positions \cite{vaswani2017attention}. The same next-token objective underlies
the dominant large-language-model interface: prompt a decoder-only model,
generate one token at a time, and optionally sample multiple completions when
the task admits several plausible reasoning paths \cite{brown2020language}.
The evaluated AR families, Qwen2.5 and Llama~3.1, use this paradigm at modern
scale \cite{qwen2024qwen25,grattafiori2024llama}. This paradigm remains strong
because it is simple to train, aligns naturally with open-ended text
continuation, and follows well-studied scaling behavior with data, parameters,
and compute \cite{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022training}. It is also supported
by mature serving infrastructure such as PagedAttention/vLLM for
high-throughput decoding \cite{kwon2023pagedattention}.

The limitations of autoregressive generation follow from the same factorization. At inference time, tokens are generated sequentially, so latency grows with output length and each decision is conditioned on a committed prefix. Stochastic decoding partly compensates for this rigidity by producing multiple candidate chains. Chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency show that repeated sampling can improve reasoning performance by aggregating final answers across sampled solutions \cite{wei2022chain,wang2023selfconsistency}. The pass@k metric, originally popularized in code-generation evaluation, formalizes the same idea as the probability that at least one of $k$ samples is correct \cite{chen2021humaneval}. Large-$k$ evaluations have also been used to reveal ranking reversals that are invisible at pass@1: in RLVR-trained reasoning models, Yue et al. report that post-trained models can lead at small $k$, while base models can overtake at large $k$ because their sampled candidate sets cover more solutions \cite{yue2025rlvr}. These results make autoregressive models the appropriate baseline for reasoning and planning, but they also show that single-sample accuracy is not sufficient to characterize these systems.

\subsection{Non-Autoregressive, Masked, and Iterative Text Generation}
Diffusion language models inherit ideas from earlier work on bidirectional and iterative generation. Masked language modeling trains a model to recover hidden tokens from both left and right context, producing strong bidirectional representations even though BERT itself is not a left-to-right generator \cite{devlin2019bert}. Conditional masked language models extended this idea to generation by repeatedly masking and replacing low-confidence tokens, allowing multiple positions to be updated in parallel rather than committing to a strict left-to-right order \cite{ghazvininejad2019maskpredict}. These methods established a key design axis that remains central today: generation can be viewed either as sequential commitment or as iterative refinement.

The masked and iterative view is especially relevant for structured outputs. Because later refinement steps can condition on partial content on both sides, non-autoregressive methods are naturally suited to infilling, editing, and constraint satisfaction. However, early masked-generation systems were typically evaluated on narrower conditional-generation tasks and did not match the scale, instruction-following ability, or infrastructure maturity of modern autoregressive LLMs. Recent diffusion-based LLMs can therefore be understood not as an isolated departure, but as a large-scale continuation of masked denoising and iterative refinement.

\subsection{Diffusion-Based Language Models}
Diffusion models generate by reversing a corruption process. In text, this idea is implemented either through continuous relaxations of token embeddings or through discrete corruption processes over categorical states. D3PMs showed how structured transition matrices and absorbing mask states can extend denoising diffusion to discrete data, including text, and connect diffusion to mask-based generation \cite{austin2021structured}. Diffusion-LM demonstrated that continuous diffusion over word embeddings can support fine-grained controllable text generation through iterative denoising \cite{li2022diffusionlm}. DiffusionBERT linked the same family of ideas more directly to generative masked language modeling by training a BERT-like model to reverse a masked diffusion process \cite{he2023diffusionbert}.

Large diffusion language models scale this denoising perspective to contemporary LLM settings. LLaDA trains a large masked diffusion model from scratch with pre-training and supervised fine-tuning, showing that instruction following and benchmark performance are not inherently tied to autoregressive decoding \cite{nie2025llada}. A complementary line adapts pretrained autoregressive checkpoints into diffusion models, reducing the cost of training diffusion LMs at scale and clarifying objective-level connections between the paradigms \cite{gong2025scaling}. Dream follows this practical route: it is an open 7B diffusion LLM initialized from an autoregressive model and reports strong performance on general, mathematical, coding, and planning-style tasks, with inference modes that include arbitrary-order generation, infilling, and tunable quality--speed trade-offs \cite{ye2025dream}. Recent surveys group these developments around a recurring set of proposed advantages: bidirectional context, iterative refinement, parallel or semi-parallel decoding, controllability, and editing flexibility \cite{li2025survey,yu2025discrete}. These properties motivate evaluation, but they do not by themselves imply better reasoning or planning performance.

These advantages should be read as conditional rather than automatic. The quality and efficiency of a diffusion LM depend on the noise schedule, number of denoising steps, block length, confidence thresholding, cache strategy, model scale, and post-training procedure. In particular, the practical latency benefit of diffusion depends on whether parallel token updates compensate for repeated denoising passes and whether the inference system can reuse computation effectively.

\subsection{Comparative Studies of Autoregressive and Diffusion Language Models}
Recent comparisons have begun to move beyond the question of whether diffusion LMs can generate fluent text. Work on adapting autoregressive models into diffusion models reports competitive language modeling, reasoning, commonsense, infilling, and instruction-following results, but it also highlights why fair comparison is difficult: training data, initialization, and objectives can differ across paradigms \cite{gong2025scaling}. LLaDA and Dream similarly show that diffusion LMs can be competitive with autoregressive baselines on broad benchmark suites, while leaving open which observed gains are due to denoising, scale, data, post-training, or benchmark interface \cite{nie2025llada,ye2025dream}.

Other studies focus on dimensions that standard benchmark tables often hide. A text-embedding comparison argues that diffusion LMs' bidirectional architecture is better aligned with retrieval and long-document representation than the unidirectional attention inherited from autoregressive pre-training \cite{zhang2025embedding}. Systems-oriented analyses characterize latency and throughput trade-offs, finding that diffusion can exploit more sequence-level parallelism but may struggle with long contexts or batched serving unless decoding schedules and caching are carefully designed \cite{kim2025beyond}. Fast-dLLM addresses part of this deployment gap by introducing approximate KV caching and confidence-aware parallel decoding for diffusion LLMs, directly targeting the inference-system maturity that autoregressive models already enjoy \cite{wu2025fastdllm}. Together, these studies make the comparison multi-dimensional: generation order, objective, decoding budget, context length, controllability, latency, throughput, and evaluation protocol can each change the conclusion.

\subsection{Evaluation Gaps and Motivation for This Work}
Prior work shows that diffusion language models can generate fluent text and can be competitive on several benchmark suites. It does not fully resolve how they should be compared with autoregressive models on reasoning and planning tasks. Existing comparisons often differ in model size, tokenizer, initialization, training data, prompt format, denoising schedule, sampling budget, or inference backend. These differences make it difficult to tell whether an observed gain comes from the generation interface or from surrounding experimental choices.

Reasoning benchmarks add another complication. A single deterministic answer and a many-sample pass@k score measure different things. The first rewards the quality of one decoded solution; the second rewards the ability to produce a candidate set that contains at least one correct answer. The RLVR/base-model reversal observed by Yue et al. is one example of why this distinction matters: a model that looks weaker under top-1 evaluation can still expose more correct candidates when the sampling budget is large \cite{yue2025rlvr}. Parser behavior can also change the measured outcome, especially when a task requires a specific output format. Finally, aggregate accuracy does not show whether two models solve the same questions or different ones.

These gaps motivate the evaluation design used in this thesis. Before
interpreting a possible paradigm difference, the comparison must make the
prompt interface, denoising or sampling budget, parser behavior, and benchmark
structure explicit. This thesis therefore evaluates not only which model has
the highest score, but how the score changes when the evaluator is allowed to
inspect more sampled candidates.
