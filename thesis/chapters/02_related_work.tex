\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Autoregressive Language Models}
Autoregressive language models define a joint sequence probability through the left-to-right factorization $p(x)=\prod_t p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$. Transformer decoders made this objective scalable by combining causal self-attention with highly parallel training over token positions \cite{vaswani2017attention}. The same next-token objective underlies the dominant large-language-model interface: prompt a decoder-only model, generate one token at a time, and optionally sample multiple completions when the task admits several plausible reasoning paths \cite{brown2020language,qwen2024qwen25,grattafiori2024llama}. This paradigm remains strong because it is simple to train, aligns naturally with open-ended text continuation, follows well-studied scaling behavior with data, parameters, and compute, and is supported by mature serving infrastructure such as PagedAttention/vLLM for high-throughput decoding \cite{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022training,kwon2023pagedattention}.

The limitations of autoregressive generation follow from the same factorization. At inference time, tokens are generated sequentially, so latency grows with output length and each decision is conditioned on an irreversible prefix. Stochastic decoding partly compensates for this rigidity by exploring multiple candidate chains. Chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency demonstrate that repeated sampling can improve reasoning performance by producing diverse intermediate solutions and aggregating their final answers \cite{wei2022chain,wang2023selfconsistency}. The pass@k metric, originally popularized in code-generation evaluation, formalizes the same idea as the probability that at least one of $k$ samples is correct \cite{chen2021humaneval}. These results make autoregressive models the appropriate baseline for reasoning and planning, but they also show that single-sample accuracy is not sufficient to characterize a generation paradigm.

\subsection{Non-Autoregressive, Masked, and Iterative Text Generation}
Diffusion language models inherit ideas from earlier work on bidirectional and iterative generation. Masked language modeling trains a model to recover hidden tokens from both left and right context, producing strong bidirectional representations even though BERT itself is not a left-to-right generator \cite{devlin2019bert}. Conditional masked language models extended this idea to generation by repeatedly masking and replacing low-confidence tokens, allowing multiple positions to be updated in parallel rather than committing to a strict left-to-right order \cite{ghazvininejad2019maskpredict}. These methods established a key design axis that remains central today: generation can be viewed either as sequential commitment or as iterative refinement.

The masked and iterative view is especially relevant for structured outputs. Because later refinement steps can condition on partial content on both sides, non-autoregressive methods are naturally suited to infilling, editing, and constraint satisfaction. However, early masked-generation systems were typically evaluated on narrower conditional-generation tasks and did not match the scale, instruction-following ability, or infrastructure maturity of modern autoregressive LLMs. Recent diffusion-based LLMs can therefore be understood not as an isolated departure, but as a large-scale continuation of masked denoising and iterative refinement.

\subsection{Diffusion-Based Language Models}
Diffusion models generate by gradually reversing a corruption process. For text, this requires either continuous relaxations of token embeddings or discrete corruption processes over categorical states. D3PMs showed how structured transition matrices and absorbing mask states can extend denoising diffusion to discrete data, including text, and connect diffusion to mask-based generation \cite{austin2021structured}. Diffusion-LM demonstrated that continuous diffusion over word embeddings can support fine-grained controllable text generation through iterative denoising \cite{li2022diffusionlm}. DiffusionBERT then linked discrete diffusion more directly to generative masked language modeling by training a BERT-like model to reverse a masked diffusion process \cite{he2023diffusionbert}.

Large diffusion language models scale this denoising perspective to contemporary LLM settings. LLaDA trains a large masked diffusion model from scratch under pre-training and supervised fine-tuning, showing that instruction following and benchmark performance are not inherently tied to autoregressive decoding \cite{nie2025llada}. A complementary line adapts pretrained autoregressive checkpoints into diffusion models, reducing the cost of training diffusion LMs at scale and clarifying objective-level connections between the paradigms \cite{gong2025scaling}. Dream follows this practical route: it is an open 7B diffusion LLM initialized from an autoregressive model and reports strong performance on general, mathematical, coding, and planning-style tasks, with inference modes that include arbitrary-order generation, infilling, and tunable quality--speed trade-offs \cite{ye2025dream}. Recent surveys synthesize these developments and emphasize the recurring advantages claimed for diffusion LMs: bidirectional context, iterative refinement, parallel or semi-parallel decoding, controllability, and editing flexibility \cite{li2025survey,yu2025discrete}.

These advantages should be read as conditional rather than automatic. The quality and efficiency of a diffusion LM depend on the noise schedule, number of denoising steps, block length, confidence thresholding, cache strategy, model scale, and post-training procedure. In particular, the practical latency benefit of diffusion depends on whether parallel token updates compensate for repeated denoising passes and whether the inference system can reuse computation effectively.

\subsection{Comparative Studies of Autoregressive and Diffusion Language Models}
Recent comparisons have begun to move beyond the question of whether diffusion LMs can generate fluent text. Work on adapting autoregressive models into diffusion models reports competitive language modeling, reasoning, commonsense, infilling, and instruction-following results, but also highlights the difficulty of fair comparison because training data, initialization, and objectives can differ across paradigms \cite{gong2025scaling}. LLaDA and Dream similarly show that diffusion LMs can be competitive with autoregressive baselines on broad benchmark suites, while leaving open which observed gains are due to denoising, scale, data, post-training, or benchmark interface \cite{nie2025llada,ye2025dream}.

Other studies focus on dimensions that standard benchmark tables often hide. A text-embedding comparison argues that diffusion LMs' bidirectional architecture is better aligned with retrieval and long-document representation than the unidirectional attention inherited from autoregressive pre-training \cite{zhang2025embedding}. Systems-oriented analyses characterize the latency and throughput trade-offs between autoregressive and diffusion LMs, finding that diffusion can exploit more sequence-level parallelism but may struggle with long contexts or batched serving unless decoding schedules and caching are carefully designed \cite{kim2025beyond}. Fast-dLLM addresses part of this deployment gap by introducing approximate KV caching and confidence-aware parallel decoding for diffusion LLMs, directly targeting the inference-system maturity that autoregressive models already enjoy \cite{wu2025fastdllm}. Together, these studies suggest that the comparison is multi-dimensional: generation order, objective, decoding budget, context length, controllability, latency, throughput, and parser/evaluation protocol can each change the conclusion.

\subsection{Evaluation Gaps and Motivation for This Work}
The existing literature establishes diffusion language models as a credible alternative to autoregressive LLMs, but it does not fully settle how the paradigms should be compared for reasoning and planning. Reported results often vary in model size, tokenizer, initialization, training data, prompt format, denoising schedule, sampling budget, and inference stack. Even when benchmark averages are reported, they rarely separate deterministic accuracy from many-sample pass@k behavior, and they typically do not ask whether the two paradigms solve the same individual questions. This matters because sampling-based evaluation can reward diversity, while deterministic evaluation rewards the quality of the first decoded solution.

This work addresses that gap through a controlled comparison of LLaDA, Dream, Qwen2.5, and Llama~3.1 on GSM8K, Countdown-cd4, and Trip Planning \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k,xie2024travelplanner}. The study holds the evaluation pipeline fixed where possible, reports both deterministic and stochastic pass@k results, treats prompt templating and parser behavior as measurement variables, and analyzes per-question correctness correlations. The goal is therefore not to claim that diffusion models are categorically better or worse than autoregressive models, but to identify which conclusions are stable under matched prompts, parsers, sample budgets, and planning benchmarks.
