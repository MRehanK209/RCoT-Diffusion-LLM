\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The initial motivation for this thesis made diffusion LLMs attractive for reasoning because iterative refinement could, in principle, avoid some irreversible mistakes of left-to-right decoding. Chain-of-thought and planning tasks often require global consistency across a generated solution, and masked denoising offers a different interface from committing to one prefix token at a time. This motivation remains useful, but the experiments show that it should be treated as a hypothesis about evaluation regimes rather than as an expectation of general diffusion superiority.

The results partially support the low-budget part of that hypothesis. On the planning-oriented tasks, diffusion models often produce stronger deterministic or pass@1 solutions. Countdown-cd4 is the cleanest case, and Trip Planning extends the pattern to a more structured natural-language output format. This suggests that iterative denoising can be competitive when the evaluation rewards the first or low-budget candidate, especially when the task has strong global validity constraints.

The contrary result is that autoregressive models gain more from large-$k$ sampling. Once the evaluator allows many stochastic attempts, AR models can explore broader candidate sets and overtake on the planning tasks. The diversity analysis is consistent with this interpretation: on Countdown Base, LLaDA averages $1.31$ unique normalized answers per question, while Qwen averages $44.65$. This does not prove the internal mechanism, but it shows why deterministic and many-sample evaluations can rank the same models differently.

The main conclusion is therefore not that diffusion beats AR or that AR beats diffusion. It is that the preferred paradigm depends on the evaluation budget and deployment objective. If only one or a few solutions can be generated, diffusion models are attractive on the evaluated planning tasks. If many attempts can be sampled and checked, autoregressive models can expose an advantage through pass@k growth. GSM8K reinforces a different point: saturated benchmarks can hide this trade-off and instead mainly reveal prompt and parser sensitivity.

The practical implication is complementarity. A low-budget system might prefer diffusion candidates because they can be coherent early, while a search-heavy system might prefer AR sampling because it produces more diverse attempts. A hybrid system could use diffusion models to propose coherent candidates and AR models, verifiers, or parsers to diversify, repair, or select among solutions. The oracle-union results show that complementary correct samples exist, but the reported gains are upper bounds rather than deployable ensemble performance.

Several threats limit this interpretation. Diffusion runs use fast-dLLM while AR runs use vLLM, so the study should not be read as a controlled latency comparison. Parser effects are especially important for Trip Planning, where malformed output can dominate the measured score. Prompt formatting changes the behavior of instruction-tuned checkpoints, and finite sample budgets mean close pass@k differences should not be overinterpreted. Finally, no learned selector, verifier, or router is implemented, so the oracle analysis motivates future hybrid systems without demonstrating one.
