\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main conclusion is protocol dependence. The paper does not show that one paradigm universally dominates; it shows that AR-vs-diffusion comparisons are highly sensitive to sampling budget, prompting, parser behavior, and evaluation protocol. Under low-budget evaluation, diffusion models are often competitive or stronger on the planning tasks; under larger pass@k budgets, autoregressive models can overtake because repeated sampling exposes a broader candidate set. GSM8K, Countdown-cd4, and Trip Planning therefore do not support a single paradigm ranking independent of the measurement setup.

Sampling budget is the clearest axis along which the conclusion changes. Deterministic accuracy and pass@1 ask whether the first decoded solution is good, while pass@k asks whether the model can generate at least one correct candidate after repeated sampling. The Countdown results show that these are different questions, and Trip Planning shows that the same distinction remains relevant when the output is a structured natural-language plan. This implies that future AR-vs-diffusion comparisons should report curves or multiple operating points rather than a single headline score.

Prompt and parser sensitivity further complicate the comparison. Instruction-tuned models are evaluated through an interface, not only through their weights: flat prompts and chat-templated prompts can expose different behavior from the same checkpoint. Likewise, parser-controlled scoring means that measured accuracy includes output-format compliance. This is especially important for Trip Planning, where malformed itineraries can dominate the error profile. These effects do not invalidate the results, but they narrow what each score can be said to measure.

The concentration-versus-diversity account should be treated as a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism. The diversity results are consistent with the idea that diffusion decoding concentrates probability mass more strongly, while autoregressive sampling explores more varied trajectories. The clearest supporting statistic is Countdown Base, where LLaDA averages $1.31$ unique normalized answers per question and Qwen averages $44.65$. This pattern could also reflect prompt format, parser validity, block-wise decoding, or backend-specific behavior, so it should be tested by varying diffusion temperature, denoising steps, block length, and inference backend, and by measuring semantic and trajectory-level diversity rather than final-answer diversity alone.

The correlation and oracle analyses suggest cross-paradigm complementarity, but only as an upper bound. The oracle union assumes an ideal selector that knows which sampled output is correct; it is not a deployable ensemble. The Trip Planning instruct oracle gain of $6.5$ percentage points shows that complementary correct samples exist, not that a practical system can recover them. Turning this into an actual hybrid method would require a learned router or selector, confidence calibration across paradigms, parser-aware validation, cost-aware sampling, and held-out validation to avoid oracle overfitting.
