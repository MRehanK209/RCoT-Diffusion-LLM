\section{Model Background and Thesis Hypothesis}
\label{sec:model-background}

The previous chapter positioned autoregressive and diffusion language models in
the literature. This chapter fixes the notation used for the comparison and
states the hypothesis in terms of the two generation interfaces. The goal is
not to re-review the model families, but to make clear why left-to-right
decoding and masked denoising imply different evaluation questions.

Let $c$ denote a benchmark prompt and let $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)$ denote the
completion generated for that prompt. The evaluation ultimately observes a set
of completions
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}_n(c)=\{\hat{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{y}^{(n)}\},
\end{equation}
where $n=1$ in deterministic or first-sample evaluation and $n>1$ in
many-sample evaluation. The model architecture determines how each
$\hat{y}^{(j)}$ is produced. Chapter~\ref{sec:methodology} describes how these
candidate sets are parsed and scored; this chapter focuses on the probability
model and generation process.

\subsection{Autoregressive Generation}
For an autoregressive model, a completion $y$ over vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ is
modeled conditionally on the prompt by the left-to-right factorization
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(y\mid c)
    =
    \prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(y_t \mid c,y_{<t}).
\end{equation}
In a decoder-only Transformer, this factorization is implemented with causal
self-attention \cite{vaswani2017attention}. If $H$ denotes the $T\times d$
hidden-state matrix at a given layer, a single attention head computes
\begin{equation}
    Q=HW_Q,\qquad K=HW_K,\qquad V_{\mathrm{attn}}=HW_V,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Attn}_{\mathrm{causal}}(H)
    =
    \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top+M_{\mathrm{causal}}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V_{\mathrm{attn}},
\end{equation}
where $M_{\mathrm{causal}}(i,j)=0$ for $j\leq i$ and
$M_{\mathrm{causal}}(i,j)=-\infty$ for $j>i$. The mask prevents position $i$
from attending to future tokens. Training uses teacher forcing with the
negative log-likelihood objective
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AR}}(\theta)
    =
    -\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_\theta(y_t\mid c,y_{<t}).
\end{equation}
At inference time, the generated prefix $\hat{y}_{<t}$ is fed back into the
model and the next token is chosen from
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_t \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid c,\hat{y}_{<t};\tau),
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ denotes the sampling temperature. If $z_t(v)$ is the logit assigned
to token $v$ at step $t$, temperature sampling uses
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(v\mid c,\hat{y}_{<t};\tau)
    =
    \frac{\exp(z_t(v)/\tau)}
    {\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(z_t(u)/\tau)}.
\end{equation}
Greedy decoding is the limiting case in which the most likely next token is
chosen at every step. Stochastic decoding draws multiple independent
left-to-right trajectories,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}^{(j)}
    =
    (\hat{y}^{(j)}_1,\ldots,\hat{y}^{(j)}_T),
    \qquad j=1,\ldots,n,
\end{equation}
so additional samples expand the candidate set that the evaluator can search
\cite{chen2021humaneval}. The important architectural constraint is that the
ordering of decisions is fixed by token position: once $\hat{y}_t$ has been
sampled, later steps condition on it rather than revising it directly.

\subsection{Masked Diffusion Generation}
Masked diffusion language models use a different generative interface. Let
$m$ denote a special mask token and let $s\in\{0,\ldots,S\}$ index the
diffusion step, with $s=0$ representing the clean sequence. A simple absorbing
mask corruption process replaces each token independently with probability
$\alpha_s$ \cite{austin2021structured,nie2025llada}:
\begin{equation}
    q(x_s^i \mid x_0^i)
    =
    (1-\alpha_s)\,\operatorname{ind}\{x_s^i=x_0^i\}
    +
    \alpha_s\,\operatorname{ind}\{x_s^i=m\},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_0=0$ and $\alpha_S$ is close to one. The resulting corrupted
sequence $x_s$ contains a subset of visible tokens and a subset of masked
positions.

Equivalently, the forward corruption distribution over the whole sequence is
\begin{equation}
    q(x_s\mid x_0)
    =
    \prod_{i=1}^{T}q(x_s^i\mid x_0^i),
\end{equation}
with a transition matrix that sends each clean token either to itself or to the
absorbing mask state. This factorized forward process is simple; the modeling
work is in learning the reverse denoising distribution.

The denoising network predicts the clean token at each masked position from
the whole partially observed sequence. Its self-attention is not constrained by
a causal mask; for a hidden-state matrix $H$, the corresponding attention head
has the form
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Attn}_{\mathrm{full}}(H)
    =
    \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V_{\mathrm{attn}}.
\end{equation}
The model distribution at position $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(x_0^i \mid x_s,s)
    =
    \operatorname{softmax}(f_\theta(x_s,s)_i),
\end{equation}
and the masked-denoising objective can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dLLM}}(\theta)
    =
    \operatorname{E}_{x_0,s,x_s}
    \left[
    -\sum_{i:x_s^i=m}\log p_\theta(x_0^i\mid x_s,s)
    \right].
\end{equation}
Here \(s\) is sampled from the training-time noise schedule and \(x_s\) is
sampled from the forward corruption process \(q(x_s \mid x_0)\).
Unlike the autoregressive loss, this objective scores multiple masked positions
conditioned on the same partially observed sequence. The conditioning context
can include tokens on both sides of a masked position whenever those tokens are
visible at step $s$.

Generation reverses the corruption process. Starting from a fully or mostly
masked sequence $x_S$, the model predicts token distributions for the masked
positions and commits a subset $U_s$ of them at each reverse step:
\begin{equation}
    x_{s-1}^i =
    \begin{cases}
    \hat{x}_0^i, & i\in U_s,\ \hat{x}_0^i\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x_s,s),\\
    x_s^i, & i\notin U_s.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Under this decoding abstraction, positions selected into \(U_s\) are committed,
while the remaining masked positions can still be refined in later steps. The
contrast with autoregressive decoding is therefore not unlimited backtracking,
but the ability to choose and fill multiple positions using bidirectional
visible context.
The subset $U_s$ may be chosen by a schedule, by block structure, or by model
confidence, depending on the implementation \cite{nie2025llada,ye2025dream}.
The corresponding reverse kernel can be summarized as
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(x_{s-1}\mid x_s)
    =
    \prod_{i\in U_s}p_\theta(x_{s-1}^i\mid x_s,s)
    \prod_{i\notin U_s}\operatorname{ind}\{x_{s-1}^i=x_s^i\}.
\end{equation}
The practical generation configuration therefore includes generation length,
block length, the number of denoising steps, and the schedule
$U_S,\ldots,U_1$. Instead of committing permanently to each left-to-right token,
the model can update a block of positions using broader sequence context. The
contrast with AR decoding is not that one process is automatically better, but
that the two processes organize test-time computation differently:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AR:}\quad t=1\rightarrow T,
    \qquad
    \mathrm{dLLM:}\quad s=S\rightarrow 0,\quad U_s\subseteq\{1,\ldots,T\}.
\end{equation}
This interface may concentrate computation on refining a candidate sequence,
which motivates testing whether the evaluated diffusion configurations behave differently under
deterministic, low-budget, and high-budget pass@k evaluation.

\subsection{Models Under Study}
LLaDA and Dream represent the masked diffusion setting
\cite{nie2025llada,ye2025dream}. Their inference depends on generation length,
block length, and the number of denoising steps. Qwen2.5 and Llama~3.1
represent the autoregressive setting \cite{qwen2024qwen25,grattafiori2024llama}.
Their main stochastic runs use vLLM sampling with a fixed generation length and
temperature, without a diffusion block or denoising schedule.

The hypothesis tested in the experiments follows from this contrast. Because
diffusion decoding fills or refines multiple positions through an iterative
process, it may produce different first-sample and low-budget behavior on tasks
that require several constraints to be satisfied at once. Any low-budget
advantage may disappear when many samples are available, because autoregressive
models can use repeated stochastic decoding to expand the candidate set. Prior
large-$k$ work on RLVR and base models shows that weaker pass@1 performance can
coexist with stronger candidate-set coverage at high $k$ \cite{yue2025rlvr}.
This thesis tests whether the evaluated diffusion configurations show a similar
large-$k$ coverage effect. The evaluation therefore tracks performance across
the sampling budget instead of reporting only one accuracy number.
